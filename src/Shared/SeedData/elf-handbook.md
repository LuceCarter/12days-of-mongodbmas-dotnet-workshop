# The Elf Handbook

*Santa's Workshop, North Pole. Ninth edition. Keep it in your apron pocket.*

## Toy Safety
Audience: toy-makers

Every toy leaves this workshop safe for the child it is meant for, and "safe" begins with the age on the label. The age on the label is the youngest age the toy is for. It is never a guess, and it is never rounded down to make a toy easier to sell.

Any toy meant for a child under three must pass the small-parts test. Drop each loose part into the brass test tube that hangs by the sanding bench. The tube is about the size of a walnut. If a part fits inside, it is a small part, and a toy with small parts is labelled three and over. No exceptions, not even for a very good toy.

Look for sharp edges with your fingertip, not your eyes. Run a finger along every edge, seam and corner. If it catches, sand it again. Fabric toys get a firm tug on every eye, button and seam. A stitched eye must hold against three strong pulls, and anything that comes away goes back to the bench.

Paint and varnish must be the water-based kind from the blue tins. The red tins are for reindeer harnesses only and never touch a toy. Night lights and anything that warms up are checked for heat after an hour of running: the casing should feel no warmer than a cup of milk you could comfortably drink.

If you are not sure a toy is safe, it is not safe. Put it on the grey shelf and ask the Head of Safety, who would always rather be asked.

## Quality Control
Audience: toy-makers

Making a toy and checking a toy are two different jobs, and they are never done by the same elf. Every finished toy goes to the Quality Bench, where an elf who did not make it tests it again from the beginning.

The tester works through the Quality Card. First, does the toy do what the box says it does? A rocking horse must rock, a night light must glow, and a kit must hold every part the list promises. Second, is it finished? No loose threads, no smudged paint, no splinters. Third, is it kind to hold? A toy should feel good in a small hand.

A toy that passes gets a green sticker and goes to the wrapping hall. A toy that fails gets a yellow sticker and goes back to the maker with a note that says what was wrong. Never tear the note up. The note is how the next toy gets better.

Spot checks happen every morning. The Head of Quality picks one toy from each batch at random and takes it apart. If a batch has two yellow stickers in a row, the whole batch is checked again before anything leaves the bench.

Tester elves are never ranked against each other. A good tester is a slow, careful, slightly fussy one, and the workshop is lucky to have you.

## Wrapping and Labelling
Audience: wrapping-elves

Wrapping is how a toy first says hello, so take your time. Always check the label before you pick up the paper. The label has the child's name, the toy, the age it is for and the sleigh bay it travels in. If any of these is missing, stop and ask. Never guess a name.

Fragile toys, such as anything made of glass, a snow globe or a painted ceramic, go into a nest of shredded paper inside a firm box before they go into wrapping paper. Wrap the box, never the bare toy. Mark the box with the blue snowflake so the sleigh crew knows to place it on top.

Soft toys are wrapped loosely. Squashing a stuffed stegosaurus flat to make a neat parcel is how it arrives with a bent tail. Tie ribbon only on the outside of the paper, and always cut the ribbon ends on the diagonal so they do not fray.

Use the thin gold tape for seams. The thick silver tape is for the sleigh crew's crates only, because a child's fingers cannot get it off. Every parcel gets a gift tag written in pencil first and ink second, so a slip of the pen never reaches a child.

Last of all, hold the parcel up and give it a gentle shake by your ear. If it rattles and it should not, unwrap it.

## Loading the Sleigh
Audience: sleigh-crew

The sleigh holds the whole world's presents, so the order of loading is not up to you: it is the route. The route list is pinned to the sleigh bay door every evening at four. Presents go in the reverse of the order they come out. The last house on the route goes in first, at the bottom, and the first house goes in last, at the top.

Heavy parcels go low and in the middle. The sleigh is steered by balance, and a heavy load at the back turns a gentle landing into a bump. Keep the left and right sides within one parcel's weight of each other. The crew chief checks this with the brass weighing beam before every flight, and nobody argues with the beam.

Parcels with a blue snowflake are fragile and ride on top, never under another parcel. Parcels with a red ribbon on the tag are for houses with a chimney that is no longer in use. They go in the front pocket so that Santa can find the front door instead.

Do not load wet parcels. If a parcel has been in the snow, dry it by the stove for ten minutes, and rewrap it if the paper has gone soft.

Count the parcels in, and count them again. The sleigh does not leave until the crew chief has two matching counts written in the logbook.

## Reindeer Care
Audience: stable-elves

The reindeer are colleagues, not equipment. They are fed three times a day: oats and dried lichen at dawn, a warm mash at midday, and hay with a handful of carrots at dusk. The carrots are a treat, not a meal. A reindeer who eats only carrots gets a very bad temper.

Check every reindeer's hooves each morning. Look for cracks, stones and ice balls packed between the toes. A small stone is easy to pick out with the hoof hook, but a limp that lasts more than a day goes straight to the stable vet.

A reindeer that will not eat is telling you something. Do not force food. First, check the water, because frozen troughs are the most common reason. Second, check for a sore mouth or a cough. Third, stay quietly with the animal for ten minutes, because a reindeer who is worried about a flight often settles with company. If it still refuses its next meal, call the stable vet that same day.

Harnesses are washed after every flight using only the red tins of varnish and oil, which never go near the toy workshop. Check every buckle, and replace any strap that has started to crack.

In the week before the big night, the reindeer rest. No extra training, no new routes and no loud music in the stables.

## Reading and Answering Letters
Audience: letter-elves

A letter is the most important thing that arrives at the workshop. Read every one, all the way to the end. The wish is often at the top, but the part that matters is often at the bottom, where a child has added a quiet note about a sibling, a grandparent or a fear.

Sort each letter into one of three trays. The green tray is for wish lists. The blue tray is for letters that ask a question, such as "does Santa live in the snow?", and these get a personal reply. The white tray is for letters that need a grown-up: a child who is unwell, frightened or sad. Pass white-tray letters to the Head of Letters on the same day, without reading them aloud to anyone.

Never promise a particular toy. The workshop promises care, not catalogue numbers. A good reply says that the wish has been heard and written down, and nothing more.

Keep the child's own words. If a child has described a toy as "a dinosaur that glows," write down "a dinosaur that glows", not "a night light". Their words are how we find the right toy.

Spelling is never corrected in a reply. Handwriting is never remarked on. Every letter is answered in the same warm voice, whether it came from the next village or the far side of the world.

## The Naughty and Nice Ledger
Audience: ledger-keepers

The ledger is a record, not a judgement. Every entry says what happened, when, and how much it counted for. It never says what kind of child someone is.

Each child's entries are totalled once a year, in the first week of December. A child whose total is above the line is on the Nice list. Below the line, the workshop does not send a lump of coal. It sends a note, and the Head of Ledgers calls a meeting to ask what that child might need.

Entries are never deleted. If an entry was recorded in error, add a correction beside it and keep both, so the record shows what was believed and when. An apology that arrives late is entered on the day it arrives and counts in full.

Ledger-keepers are told about children, never about their families, and only the facts needed to total an entry. If you hear something that was not meant for the ledger, forget it.

When a child's total is close to the line, the keeper re-reads every entry from the year and asks a single question about each: was this a mistake, or was this unkind? Mistakes are what childhood is for.

## Workshop Rules
Audience: all-elves

The workshop runs on a few simple rules, and they apply to every elf from the newest apprentice to the Head of Toys.

Aprons on, bells off. The little brass bells on your hat jingle in a way that hides the sound of a machine in trouble. Take them off at the bench and put them back on at the door.

Visitors are welcome in the Great Hall and the tea room, and nowhere else. A visitor may enter the toy floor only with a signed visitor card and an elf escort who stays within arm's reach for the whole visit. Children, however small, wear the green helmet.

Tools go back on their hooks, with the outline painted behind each tool showing where it lives. If you cannot see an outline, put the tool somewhere obvious and tell the tool warden.

Spills are cleaned straight away. Glitter, which has a way of getting everywhere, is swept with the damp broom and never with the dry one, because the dry one sends it into the air and onto the machines.

No running on the toy floor. Walk quickly if you must, but walk. Every elf has the right to say "stop" to a machine, a task or a colleague, and nobody is ever in trouble for using it.

## Elf Wellbeing
Audience: all-elves

A tired elf makes a poor toy. The workshop is busiest in the weeks before the big night, and that is exactly when you must look after yourself most carefully.

Everyone gets a proper break every three hours: ten minutes away from the bench, with hot chocolate from the urn in the corridor. The hot chocolate is free, and so is the sit-down. A full lunch is not a luxury and is never skipped to finish a batch.

If you feel exhausted, overwhelmed or unwell, tell your team leader or any elf wearing a purple scarf. Purple-scarf elves are trained to listen, and they will never tell you to cheer up. They will find you a quiet room, a warm blanket and, if you want it, someone to talk to. Telling them early is never a weakness.

Sleep matters more in December than at any other time. Every elf has a bunk in the dormitory and a guaranteed eight hours when the night shift ends. Nobody works a double shift. A rested elf finishes a toy faster than a tired one finishes two.

On the first of January the whole workshop closes for three days, with no exceptions and no emergency orders. You have earned it.

## Emergencies
Audience: all-elves

Stay calm and stop the machine first. Every machine on the toy floor has a big red stop button at hip height. Press it, then raise your arm so the elf nearest you knows to raise theirs.

If there is a fire, however small, ring the brass bell by the nearest door three times and leave by the nearest door. Do not stop to collect toys or tools. The meeting point is the old oak at the edge of the yard, and the team leader will count heads. Use the red extinguishers for the stoves and the blue ones for anything electrical.

If someone is hurt, do not move them unless they are in danger. Send one elf to the First Aid Room and one to the Head of Safety, and stay with the person until help comes. Speak quietly and tell them exactly what is happening.

If the lights go out, stay where you are. Every workshop door has a glow-moss lantern that wakes up in the dark, and the lantern will show you the way out. If the heating fails on a freezing night, the dormitory has spare blankets in the chest by the stove, and the Great Hall is warm.

After every emergency, large or small, a short report is written within a day. It is not to find someone to blame, but so that it never happens the same way twice.
